Listing eligibility, review procedures, key deadlines and practical considerations for the Main Board and GEM.

Information current as of 26 November 2026. The rule basis is the Main Board Listing Rules and GEM Listing Rules currently in force, together with public documents of HKEX and the SFC.

1. Executive Summary

This report sets out the complete initial public offering (IPO) process on the Main Board and GEM of Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Limited (HKEX), focusing on three questions: who can list, who does the work, and in what sequence and over what timeframe. All rule references are drawn from the Main Board Listing Rules and GEM Listing Rules currently in force and from public documents of HKEX and the SFC, with a clause index and sources listed in the appendices.

1.1 Five Key Conclusions

① Dual-track regulation. The Stock Exchange of Hong Kong (SEHK, an HKEX subsidiary) reviews listing eligibility and rule compliance; the SFC concurrently reviews disclosure through “dual filing” and may object to an application or impose conditions; the prospectus must also be registered under the Companies (Winding Up and Miscellaneous Provisions) Ordinance (Cap. 32).

② Four hard deadlines. An A1 may be filed only after the sponsor has been formally appointed for a full 2 months; an A1 remains valid for 6 months; the prospectus must be substantially finalised 4 “full business days” before the hearing; a returned application may be resubmitted only after 8 weeks.

③ Three financial tests on the Main Board. Profit test; market capitalisation/revenue/cash flow test; market capitalisation/revenue test. Pre-revenue biotech and Specialist Technology companies may follow Chapter 18A and Chapter 18C respectively.

④ Typical timeline. About 8–15 months from formal sponsor appointment to listing, and about 6–12 months from A1 submission. These are market experience figures; the rules themselves set only caps and checkpoints, not committed timelines.

⑤ Recent changes. From August 2025 public float follows a new market-cap-tiered scale; FINI has compressed IPO settlement to T+2; the market-wide T+1 target is the second half of 2028.

1.2 Scope and Methodology

  • Basis: HKEX’s electronic Main Board Listing Rules (including Chapters 3A, 8, 9, 11A, 12, 18A and 18C), Chapter 11 of the GEM Listing Rules, the Main Board Fees Rules, Practice Notes PN18/PN22, the SFC dual filing page and HKEX market information pages.
  • Conventions: unless otherwise stated, all amounts are in Hong Kong dollars; “typical duration” and “usual practice” reflect market experience and are not rule commitments; rule provisions are governed by the English text, and the Chinese wording is a paraphrase for readability.
  • As at: information is current as of 26 November 2026. Rules are amended frequently, and live transactions should rely on the version in force at the time of submission.

2. The Hong Kong IPO Market and Listing Architecture

2.1 Market Position

Hong Kong is one of the world’s leading equity fundraising markets. According to HKEX, Hong Kong ranked first globally by IPO proceeds in 2025, raising more than US$36 billion over the year; since the new economy listing regime was introduced in 2018, more than 430 new economy companies have listed in Hong Kong, raising over US$150 billion in aggregate. As at 2025, around 167 biotech companies had listed under Chapter 18A.

The direct implication for issuers is that the Hong Kong market provides two sources of capital—international institutional money and Mainland Chinese money (through the mutual access mechanisms)—and has dedicated channels for weighted voting rights, pre-revenue biotech, Specialist Technology, SPACs and other special structures, giving issuers considerable choice of board.

3. Listing Eligibility and Board Selection

3.1 The Three Financial Tests on the Main Board

A Main Board applicant must satisfy one of three tests (Rule 8.05). The “soft” conditions common to all three are a trading record of not less than three financial years, management continuity over the three most recent financial years, and ownership continuity over the most recent audited financial year.

Table 3 The Three Financial Tests on the Main Board (Rule 8.05)

Profit testProfit attributable to shareholders ≥ HK$35 million in the most recent year; ≥ HK$45 million in aggregate for the two preceding yearsProfit must arise from ordinary business and exclude non-recurring gains and losses; suited to companies with stable earnings
Market cap/revenue/cash flow testMarket cap at listing ≥ HK$2 billion; revenue in the most recent audited year ≥ HK$500 million; operating cash flow ≥ HK$100 million in aggregate for the three preceding yearsSuited to companies with large revenue and robust cash flow whose profit is affected by depreciation or expansion
Market cap/revenue testMarket cap at listing ≥ HK$4 billion; revenue in the most recent audited year ≥ HK$500 millionSuited to companies with high valuations and high revenue but insufficient current profit (waivers are addressed in Rule 8.05A)

Note: All three tests require a trading record of at least three financial years; Chapter 18A and 18C applicants may be exempt from Rule 8.05 and are assessed under their respective chapters.

3.5 Choosing a Board and Test Path

  • Stable profits, moderate size: the profit test is the most direct, with review focused on earnings quality and business sustainability.
  • Large revenue but thin profit: assess the market cap/revenue test first; where operating cash flow is sound, the market cap/revenue/cash flow test requires a lower market cap threshold (HK$2 billion versus HK$4 billion).
  • Pre-revenue biotech: Chapter 18A; the R&D progress of core products and sophisticated investors are the focus of review.
  • Specialist Technology (including AI, robotics and new materials): Chapter 18C; first determine whether the company is “commercialised” or “pre-commercial”—there is a HK$4 billion gap between the two market cap thresholds.
  • Small and medium-sized enterprises: GEM has lower thresholds, and of the two tests the cash flow test imposes no hard profit requirement, suiting companies with steady operating cash flow.

5.2 Stage 1: Preparation and Restructuring

This stage determines whether the project can pass the hearing smoothly, and its workload is often underestimated. The core task is not “writing materials” but turning the company into an entity that the capital markets can understand and the regulators can accept.

  • Determining the board and test path: first run market cap and financial projections, then work backwards to identify the applicable test; where the profit test applies, ensure that profit in the most recent year and the two preceding years all meet the thresholds, excluding one-off gains.
  • Restructuring: choosing a red-chip / H-share / VIE structure; establishing a Cayman or BVI holding platform; injecting domestic interests; unwinding nominee shareholdings; regularising employee shareholding platforms.
  • Independence and competing business: where a controlling shareholder operates a competing business, the listing document must make full disclosure under Rule 8.10 and demonstrate that the applicant can operate independently; connected transactions must be eliminated or regularised.
  • Pre-IPO investment: the timing, valuation and special rights of investors (repurchase, liquidation preference, etc.) must be properly addressed before submission and are usually required to be terminated or adjusted before listing.
  • Financials and internal controls: preparing three-year (Main Board) or two-year (GEM) statements under the applicable accounting standards (HKFRS/IFRS); revenue recognition, customer concentration and related-party fund flows are frequently queried.
  • Compliance review: licences, environmental protection, work safety, labour and social security, tax, data and cybersecurity, and intellectual property.

5.3 Stage 2: Sponsor Appointment and Due Diligence

The sponsor must be engaged under a written engagement letter and must notify SEHK in writing as soon as practicable after appointment. The key hard rule is that a listing application may not be submitted less than 2 months after the date of the sponsor’s formal appointment; where there are multiple sponsors, the clock runs from the formal appointment of the last sponsor (Rule 3A.02B). In practice this rule makes “finding a sponsor” the starting point of the project timetable.

The sponsor must conduct reasonable due diligence in accordance with Practice Note 21 (PN21) and bears statutory responsibility for material matters in the listing document. In practice the sponsor drives a closed loop of “due diligence checklist—management interviews—third-party verification—working paper archiving” while coordinating lawyers, accountants, valuers and internal control advisers.

5.4 Stage 3: A1 Submission and Dual Filing

The applicant files its listing application on Form A1, completed and signed by the sponsor on its behalf, accompanied by the documents specified in Rule 9.10A(1), the Application Proof (the draft listing document) and the initial listing fee. The rules expressly require the application to be “substantially complete”; otherwise SEHK will return the application, return the documents and deal with fees already paid in accordance with the rules; a returned application may be resubmitted no earlier than 8 weeks after the date of return (Rule 9.03(3)).

There are two points on publication: first, publication of the Application Proof is “optional”—where the applicant elects to publish, it must submit the Public AP for publication on the same day it files its application with SEHK (Rule 12.01A, PN22); second, whether or not the AP is published, the application must be dual-filed with the SFC through SEHK, and the SFC may object to the application or impose conditions under section 6 of the Securities and Futures (Stock Market Listing) Rules.

The initial listing fee is charged by tier according to the monetary value of the securities to be listed and must be paid in advance at the time of application (see Chapter 7).

5.5 Stage 4: Regulatory Review and Enquiries

Review is conducted concurrently by the SEHK Listing Division and the SFC, typically in the form of multiple rounds of written enquiries (market experience suggests 3–6 rounds, depending on project complexity). The issuer and sponsor must respond round by round and submit a revised listing document. The rules require that revisions not be “fragmented” and that each version fully address all comments on the previous version (Rule 9.03(5)); the applicant must update SEHK on the progress of its application every two weeks (Rule 9.03(2)).

There are two hard timing checkpoints:

  • Six-month validity: if listing is not completed within 6 months from the date of A1 submission, the application lapses and the initial listing fee is not refunded; to continue, a fresh application must be filed and the fee paid again (note 2 to Rule 9.03(1)).
  • Four full business days before the hearing: SEHK may require the hearing to be postponed unless, at least 4 full business days before the hearing, the applicant has submitted a revised listing document containing full disclosure and all outstanding documents, and has satisfactorily resolved the regulatory comments (Rule 9.03(4)).

Review focus is not only whether “the numbers add up” but also whether “the business is real, sustainable and suitable for listing”. Common lines of enquiry are set out in Chapter 9.

5.6 Stage 5: Listing Committee Hearing

The Listing Committee reviews the application at the hearing and decides to approve, give conditional approval, defer consideration or reject. Before the hearing, the sponsor must ensure that the listing document is final, that all waiver applications (if any) have been submitted and that directors and senior management have completed the necessary declarations.

If the application is rejected or approved with conditions, the applicant may apply for a review within the prescribed period, which is considered by the Listing Review Committee. Review is a remedial procedure and should not form part of the normal timetable.

5.7 Stage 6: Post-Hearing Launch and Listing

Once the hearing is passed, the project enters the launch execution phase, with the pace driven by the market window.

  • Publication of the PHIP: after receiving the “post-hearing letter” from SEHK and confirming that the principal comments have been addressed, the applicant must publish the Post Hearing Information Pack on the HKEX website as soon as practicable; this is mandatory (Rule 12.01B, PN22).
  • Finalising and registering the prospectus: the prospectus must comply with the Listing Rules and may be issued only after registration with the Companies Registry under the Companies (Winding Up and Miscellaneous Provisions) Ordinance (Rule 11A.01). It may not be issued before SEHK confirms that it has no further comments on the listing document (Rule 9.07).
  • Roadshow and bookbuilding: management roadshow, pre-deal analyst discussions and institutional price discovery; the OC coordinates the order book.
  • Public offer and placing: the public offer portion typically accounts for about 10% of the global offering (subject to upward adjustment on clawback), with the placing accounting for the balance; the public offer is divided by application amount into Pool A (HK$5 million and below) and Pool B (above HK$5 million), with the two pools allocated equally (PN18). Where over-subscription reaches the agreed multiples, clawback applies in accordance with the mechanism disclosed in the prospectus.
  • Pricing, allotment and settlement: allocation is determined after pricing and the allotment results are announced; the FINI platform handles subscription monies and share settlement, and the IPO settlement cycle has been shortened from T+5 to T+2.
  • Listing: trading in the shares begins on SEHK; during the stabilisation period (typically 30 days) the over-allotment option (greenshoe, up to 15%) may be exercised; controlling shareholders must comply with lock-up undertakings.

Putting these checkpoints together, a reasonably smooth project roughly follows this rhythm (experience-based):

  • T-12 to T-6 months: restructuring, audit, internal control remediation and engaging intermediaries.
  • T-2 months: formal sponsor appointment (no A1 submission before this point).
  • T0: A1 submission, same-day (optional) AP publication, dual filing.
  • T+1 to T+5 months: multiple enquiry rounds and listing document revisions; where financial information is approaching the 6-month validity limit, consider a stub-period audit.
  • T+5 to T+6 months: hearing, PHIP, registration, roadshow and pricing, launch.
  • Around T+6 months: listing. Where there are many enquiry rounds or additional audit is needed, slippage to 9–12 months overall is common.

It should be emphasised that the 6-month validity of the A1 is a “countdown”, not a “target”. Once a stub-period audit or slow enquiries make completion within 6 months impossible, the project must be resubmitted with the fee paid again, significantly increasing the time cost.

8. Offering and Pricing Mechanisms

8.1 Offering Structure

  • Public offer: aimed at retail investors, typically 10% of the global offering initially; over-subscription triggers the clawback mechanism, with the clawback percentage applied according to the tiered scale disclosed in the prospectus (commonly 15x, 50x and 100x tiers).
  • Placing: aimed at institutional and professional investors, coordinated by the OC through bookbuilding; the placing portion typically accounts for 90%.
  • Pool A/Pool B: the public offer portion is divided equally into two pools, with Pool A for applicants applying for HK$5 million or less and Pool B for those applying for more than HK$5 million; where one pool is under-subscribed, the surplus is transferred to the other pool (PN18).
  • Cornerstone investors: institutional investors that commit to subscribe before listing, usually subject to a 6-month lock-up; an excessively high cornerstone allocation squeezes the public offer portion and must be disclosed in the prospectus.
  • Over-allotment option (greenshoe): underwriters may over-allot up to 15% of the shares during the stabilisation period to dampen post-listing volatility.

8.2 Settlement and Clearing

FINI (Fast Interface for New Issuance) is HKEX’s IPO settlement platform, launched in November 2023, which compresses the settlement cycle from pricing to listing from T+5 to T+2, significantly reducing market and credit risk during the offering period. Issuers, sponsors, receiving banks, registrars and HKSCC all coordinate through the platform.

A longer-term change is the migration of the cash market as a whole to T+1: HKEX published its consultation conclusions in October 2026, targeting implementation in the second half of 2028, subject to market readiness and regulatory approval. The direct implication for IPO projects is that launch execution and settlement processes will be compressed again, and issuers and intermediaries need to adjust their internal processes in advance.

9. Review Focus and Common Issues

SEHK and SFC enquiries are not random; the vast majority fall into the following categories. Preparing in advance can significantly reduce the number of enquiry rounds.

9.1 Business and Suitability

  • Business substance: revenue recognition policies, customer and supplier concentration, abnormal gross margins, related-party transactions, and consistency between fund flows and contracts.
  • Sustainability: industry cycles, policy dependence, risk of losing major customers, sources of core technology and ownership of intellectual property.
  • Suitability: management integrity, historical equity defects, administrative penalties, controlling shareholder background; SEHK may reject an application on the ground of “unsuitability for listing”.

9.2 Structure and Independence

  • Competing business: where a controlling shareholder operates a competing business, disclosure must be made under Rule 8.10 of the reasons for exclusion, the scale of the competing business and evidence of independence.
  • Connected transactions: continuing connected transactions after listing must comply with Chapter 14A; unnecessary related-party dealings must be eliminated before listing.
  • VIE structure: validity of the contractual arrangements, compliance with foreign exchange and foreign investment access rules, and adequacy of risk disclosure.

9.3 Financials and Internal Controls

  • Reporting accountant’s report: three financial years on the Main Board and two on GEM; the latest financial period must not be more than 6 months before the listing document.
  • Internal controls: the degree to which processes such as treasury management, procurement and sales, inventory and approval of connected transactions are institutionalised; internal control adviser reports are often required.
  • Tax and social security: historical underpayments, reliance on tax incentives and transfer pricing on cross-border transactions.

9.4 Offering and Public Float

  • Public float and free float: the market-cap-tiered public float percentage, 300 shareholders, the 50% cap on the top three public shareholders and the free float threshold—failure on any one of these prevents listing.
  • Independence of cornerstones and the underwriting syndicate: independence of cornerstone investors, source of subscription funds and whether any side agreements exist.
  • Pre-IPO investment: fairness of the entry price, whether special rights have been terminated, and whether the investment amounts to last-minute pre-IPO shareholding.

10. Rule Changes in 2025–2026

  • New public float and free float framework (effective 4 August 2025): the flat 25% requirement was replaced by market-cap tiers (25%/15%/10%), and a free float requirement was introduced (≥10% and market cap ≥ HK$50 million, or market cap ≥ HK$600 million). This is more flexible for large issuers but places greater emphasis on genuinely tradable shares for small and medium-sized issuers.
  • Optional publication of the Application Proof: the current Rule 12.01A is framed as “if the applicant elects to publish”; where the applicant elects to publish, the AP must be submitted on the same day as the A1. Applicants that do not elect to publish must still publish a PHIP after the hearing.
  • Chapter 18C market cap and revenue thresholds: commercialised Specialist Technology companies—market cap at listing of not less than HK$6 billion and Specialist Technology revenue of not less than HK$250 million in the most recent audited year; pre-commercial companies—market cap of not less than HK$10 billion, with no revenue requirement.
  • FINI enhancements: a round of functional upgrades in 2025 further reduced manual steps in launch execution.
  • T+1 consultation conclusions (October 2026): the cash market targets T+1 implementation in the second half of 2028, subject to market readiness and regulatory approval; T+2 remains in place for now.

11. Practical Recommendations

  • Treat sponsor appointment as the T-2 starting point: because of the statutory 2-month interval, the later the sponsor is in place, the later the A1 is filed; start sponsor selection as soon as the restructuring plan has largely taken shape.
  • Fix “hard defects” before discussing valuation: competing business, connected transactions, equity defects and missing licences are structural issues; leaving them to the enquiry stage often leads to hearing delays or even withdrawal.
  • Front-load audit and internal controls: the three-year (or two-year) financial statements and internal control remediation sit on the longest critical path and should run in parallel with, not after, restructuring.
  • Plan backwards from the 6-month validity period: convert enquiry rounds, stub-period audits, hearing scheduling and the market window into weeks, and build in a buffer of at least 4–6 weeks.
  • Enquiry responses should be “complete” rather than “fast”: the rules expressly prohibit fragmented revisions; each version of the listing document should address all comments in one go, otherwise a redo may be required.
  • Design the offering structure and public float together: offering size, secondary share sales, cornerstone allocation and the clawback mechanism jointly determine whether the public float and free float thresholds are met, and should be modelled before submission.
  • Watch the market window: passing review does not guarantee a successful launch; pricing and demand depend on the window, and where necessary it is better to delay the offering than to price reluctantly.
